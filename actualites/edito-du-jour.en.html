Blue Soft weaves its regional web, one city at a time

Source: lembarque.com →

📬 Subscribe to the Cybersecurity newsletter

Blue Soft is a French digital services group founded in 2008, which supports companies in six areas: business applications, cybersecurity and compliance, data and AI, infrastructure, digital work environments, and change management. It employs 950 people across nine cities in France and Switzerland, and generated 106 million euros in revenue in 2025. Its target, Roxys, is an SME created in 2012 in Arras, Pas-de-Calais, which reported 3.28 million euros in revenue in 2025. Roxys specializes in the Microsoft ecosystem — identity, security, collaborative work, workstation management, and change management — and works exclusively with organizations. The transaction, effective since October 20, 2026, was carried out for an undisclosed amount.

The surface reading: Blue Soft strengthens its Microsoft expertise and establishes a foothold in Hauts-de-France, a region previously absent from its network.

What deserves attention is the underlying model. Blue Soft recently acquired Wemoov in Carquefou, near Nantes; now comes Arras. The strategy is not to acquire a rare capability or a unique asset — it is to acquire geographic presence. In digital services, the mid-market client or local authority does not just choose expertise; they choose someone who can be there. The value Blue Soft is building is not in a proprietary product but in a network of regional agencies capable of responding to local tenders that a centralized Parisian group cannot credibly compete for. Roxys brings its Microsoft experts, certainly — but above all, it brings Arras, its clients, and its territorial legitimacy.

For a mid-sized digital services group, the real barrier to entry is not technical: it's capillarity. Blue Soft is building this barrier, deal after deal, city after city.

Rainbow Premium: ALE sells sovereignty, not functionality

Source: al-enterprise.com →

ALE (Alcatel-Lucent Enterprise), with operational headquarters in Colombes, markets unified communications solutions for businesses and the public sector under the brand Alcatel-Lucent Enterprise. Its Rainbow platform is a collaborative communications environment (messaging, videoconferencing, telephony). The press release describes a product launch, not an acquisition; no amount is associated with this operation.

The positioning is explicit: Rainbow Premium targets public sector, healthcare, and defense organizations that need to adopt AI while remaining compliant with European data regulations and the European Commission's sovereign cloud framework — and that want to disengage from exposure to the US Cloud Act. Features included in the Premium license are meeting summaries, voice transcription, live translation, and document translation, all processed in European data centers.

What ALE is selling here is not AI that performs better than its American competitors — it's the certainty that data will not leave Europe. In a context where hospitals, ministries, and defense industries are accelerating their disengagement from infrastructures subject to foreign jurisdictions, the sovereignty has become a commercial argument in its own right, and no longer just a regulatory constraint. The European sovereign cloud market is projected to grow by 83% year-on-year according to Gartner: ALE is positioning itself in this flow before European competition becomes structured.

Bpifrance enters N4 Group: the BlueSpring fund makes its mark

Source: mesinfos.fr →

N4 Group is an Isère-based family group created in Voiron in 1999, owning 14 brands in sports equipment and nutrition, including FitnessBoutique. The group completed its IPO on Euronext Growth Paris on October 21, 2026, raising 33.1 million euros — including 7 million in capital increase and 26.1 million in sales of existing shares — for a valuation of 267 million euros.

N4 Group plans to allocate 70% of the funds from the new share issue to strengthening its industrial capacities at its Voiron site (N4 Labs, new production lines) and 30% to its development in Europe, primarily in Belgium, Spain, and Italy.

A useful context note: the initial offering aimed for 50 million euros, including 43 million in disposals. BlueSpring's entry fits into this framework — the fund supports small-cap listed companies in their transformation phases, with a stated long-term vocation.

For N4 Group, the stake taken by Bpifrance is less about the amount than the signal it sends: a reference institutional shareholder stabilizes the free float of a stock still little known to the markets and lends credibility to the European growth trajectory for future investors.

AQQI: real estate purchase as an organizational problem, not a search problem

Source: jaimelesstartups.fr →

📬 Subscribe to the Construction & PropTech newsletter

AQQI is a French startup founded in early 2026 by Alexandre Ducoeur Martins and Julie de la Porte des Vaux, both former employees of Studapart — a real estate rental platform sold for over 55 million euros. AQQI is developing a personal AI assistant dedicated to real estate purchases: the tool is designed to understand the buyer's project, track its evolution over time, and handle the administrative and relational steps of the process — follow-ups, visit organization, information gathering, coordination with professionals. According to the company, buyers spend an average of 150 hours on their project and exchange over a hundred times by email and phone; AQQI claims to reduce the time-consuming tasks of this process by 75%.

The startup announced a fundraising of 4 million euros in pre-seed, led by Axeleo and Aquiti, with the participation of Kima Ventures, of Pierre Kosciusko-Morizet and of 50 Partners. The solution is being tested with several dozen users in Île-de-France, Bordeaux, and Marseille; deployment is planned for 1,000 buyers before a public launch in early 2027.

What distinguishes AQQI's positioning from listing portals or property search tools is the entry point: AQQI does not seek to help the buyer find the right property — it seeks to prevent them from getting bogged down in the process once they have decided to search. It is a project management assistant, not a search engine. The addressable market is large — every buyer in France — but monetization and retention remain to be proven: a real estate purchase is a rare event in a lifetime, which requires either a transaction-based model or an extension to other stages of the journey (financing, renovations, resale).

The founders' credibility in the real estate market is real, and the pre-seed ticket is consistent with the stage. The challenge in the coming months will be less about convincing users than about demonstrating a conversion rate and value high enough to justify the acquisition cost in a market where buyers do not behave like subscribers.

Atlas Bio: simulating clinical trials before launching them

Source: tech-insider.org →

📬 Subscribe to the HealthTech & Digital Health newsletter

Atlas Bio is a Basel-based startup building an AI model capable of simulating clinical trials — what researchers call in silico trials — to predict, even before recruiting a single patient, how a drug candidate will behave in Phase II, at the individual level rather than a population average. The problem it tackles is quantifiable and brutal: approximately 70% of drugs fail in Phase II, the stage where human trials truly begin, after years and hundreds of millions spent in preclinical development.

The company announced 12.5 million dollars in total funding, comprising a 10 million dollars seed round led by Creandum — whose partner Katharina Wilhelm joins the board of directors — and a previously raised 2.5 million dollars pre-seed round led by First Momentum Ventures and LEA Partners, revealed simultaneously. The funds will be used to strengthen scientific and commercial teams, deepen platform validation, and expand the corporate client base by the end of 2026 — Atlas Bio already describes itself as selling to key accounts.

The surface reading: a deeptech biotech startup raises a modest amount in a week dominated by AI infrastructure checks of a completely different caliber.

The twist lies in the nature of the problem. Phase II clinical trials are not just costly — they are the primary destroyer of value for the entire global pharmaceutical industry. Each failure at this stage represents years of R&D and hundreds of millions swallowed. What Atlas Bio offers is not a productivity tool: it's a filter placed before the abyss. If the platform can predict with sufficient reliability which candidates will fail — and which deserve to advance — it is not selling software; it is selling insurance on research capital. The business model targets pharmaceutical laboratories and biotechs that invest in molecule pipelines: for them, paying for a simulated trial is rational as long as it reduces the probability of burning ten times more in a real trial.

The real validation challenge is technical: an AI model for clinical trials is only as good as its training data and its ability to generalize to new molecules. The question that key account clients will ask is not “does your AI predict well?” but “what data did you train on, and how do you prove it holds for my specific molecule?”

For an industrial partner or a corporate pharmaceutical investor, Atlas Bio represents exactly the type of asymmetric bet that the ticket size makes accessible: if the validation holds, the value created is immeasurable compared to the 12.5 million committed.
