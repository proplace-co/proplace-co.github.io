Deploiable: an AI product factory designed to be acquired, not to dominate

Source: dealroom.co →

Deploiable is a product lab based in Rome that develops artificial intelligence applications targeting the concrete needs of small and medium-sized enterprises. Its model is unusual: rather than seeking to become a dominant platform, it designs sectoral products conceived from the outset to be acquired by intermediate-sized companies. The team calls these exits “micro-exits.”

The company raised €500,000 in seed funding, with the fund Cannonball as the lead investor, and participation from business angels Claudio Enriquez, Enrico Reggiani, and Mirko Faiozzo. The three co-founders — Roberto Macina (CEO, software engineer), Valentina Iannucci (strategy), and Alessandro Piccinini (deployment) — all sit on the board.

The obvious interpretation: a micro seed round for another AI lab, in a European ecosystem still lagging behind the United States in terms of funding and exits.

What deserves attention is elsewhere. Roberto Macina has already done this twice: Qurami, his queue management startup founded in 2010, was acquired by ufirst in 2018; then the venture builder Wda, co-founded with Mario Costanzo, was acquired by the Next4 holding company at the end of 2024. This is not a founder who theorizes exits — this is someone who has practiced it, twice, on a small scale.

His diagnosis is precise: European startups and industrial companies “do not speak the same financial language.” The former aim for growth metrics that do not correspond to the acquisition criteria of the latter. Deploiable proposes to short-circuit this discrepancy by designing products whose metrics and revenues are calibrated to convince a modest industrial buyer, not a growth fund.

This bet is, implicitly, a lucid admission about the structure of the European market: where the American ecosystem can absorb hundreds of AI startups in mega-rounds and IPOs, Europe needs a human-scale exit infrastructure — and no one has truly industrialized it yet. If the model works, it does not produce a unicorn; it perhaps produces something more useful to the European economic fabric: a transmission chain between AI and SMEs that have neither the means nor the appetite to develop these tools themselves.

For a French investor or executive: the “product lab with micro-exits” model remains almost absent from the French ecosystem, where startup culture is still oriented towards Series B-C fundraising or acquisition by a large group. The question Deploiable indirectly poses is whether French mid-sized companies are ready to acquire AI components worth a few hundred thousand euros — and whether support structures could facilitate these transactions which, today, have neither investment bankers nor standardized processes.
